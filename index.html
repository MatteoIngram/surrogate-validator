Surrogate data test for return series

Tests whether a return series, or a strategy's returns, contains structure that noise with the same distribution and autocorrelation would not produce.

BDS, Lyapunov and Sharpe ratio statistics compared against IAAFT surrogates. Method and limits.

Your data never leaves your browser. The computation runs locally in Python (Pyodide); the file is read on this page and nothing is uploaded.

Data

CSV with columns date, return and, for the strategy test, position. Returns are simple or log returns as decimals, one row per period, oldest first, at least 500 rows. In strategy mode the position on a row is the exposure held over that row's return. Download example files.

Results

What this tests

Daily returns are not plain noise. Their distribution has fat tails and their autocorrelation is rarely exactly zero, so a comparison with Gaussian noise finds "structure" almost every time and says nothing. This tool asks a narrower question: does the series contain anything that noise with the same distribution and the same linear autocorrelation would not also produce?

A surrogate is a synthetic series made by scrambling the data while keeping its histogram and its power spectrum. Think of re-cutting a film: every frame is still there, and the overall pace of cuts is kept, but the plot is gone. Any statistic that comes out the same on the real series and on its surrogates cannot be evidence of a plot.

Each statistic is computed on the data and on 199 surrogates. The p-value is the share of surrogates at least as extreme as the data. A p-value of 0.01 means only about 1% of surrogates were as extreme; it is not the probability that the series is random.

A rejection means the series has structure beyond its distribution and linear autocorrelation. It does not mean that structure is predictable or tradable. In daily returns it is usually volatility clustering, which is why the tests are repeated on GARCH residuals. Not rejecting means this method found nothing at this sample size. It does not prove the series is random.

Limits: results depend on sample length and embedding parameters. Structural breaks and other non-stationarity can produce rejections that are not nonlinearity. The strategy test takes positions as given and cannot see how they were chosen, beyond the number of variants you report.

Methodology

  1. Surrogates are generated by the iterative amplitude-adjusted Fourier transform (IAAFT; Schreiber & Schmitz, 1996), 100 iterations or until the ranks stop changing. Inference follows the surrogate-data framework of Theiler et al. (1992): the p-value is the rank of the observed statistic among the surrogates, with a minimum of 1/(N+1).
  2. Volatility is filtered with a constant-mean GARCH(1,1) model with Student-t innovations, estimated by maximum likelihood. Tests are run on the raw returns and on the standardised residuals.
  3. The BDS statistic (Brock et al., 1996) is computed for embedding dimensions 2 to 5 with ε = 1.5 standard deviations. The surrogate test uses the mean statistic over dimensions; asymptotic p-values per dimension are reported for reference.
  4. The largest Lyapunov exponent is estimated by the method of Rosenstein et al. (1993), with Theiler window 20 and a 10-step fit. The delay τ is the first minimum of average mutual information and m the first dimension with under 1% false nearest neighbours. The test is two-sided: on the Lorenz system the observed exponent lies below the surrogates.
  5. Series mode reports an overall verdict after Holm correction across its four tests.
  6. Strategy mode compares the annualised Sharpe ratio with two nulls: the same positions on IAAFT surrogates of the returns, and the positions shuffled in time. It also reports the deflated Sharpe ratio (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014), with the cross-trial Sharpe variance estimated from the permutation null.

Validation on known processes: Gaussian white noise rejects in 2% of 50 runs, simulated GARCH(1,1) data rejects on raw BDS in 20 of 20 runs and on residual BDS in none, and the Lorenz system rejects on all four tests. See the repository README for details.

  • date: one row per period, oldest first. YYYY-MM-DD is safest; M/D/YYYY also works.
  • return: a decimal return per period, so 1% is 0.01. Prices must be converted to returns first.
  • position: the exposure held over that row's return, for example 1 long, 0 flat, −1 short, or fractions. Decide it with data up to the previous row to avoid look-ahead.
  • No empty cells, no repeated dates, at least 500 rows. The first line must be the header.
